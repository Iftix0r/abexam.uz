Whereas today we use plaster and paint or wallpaper, the Iron Age people had a mixture of clay, cow dung and straw, which they daubed all over the inside and outside to give the house a smooth finish. Part 4. You will hear part of a talk about recent research into the design of workplaces. First you have some time to look at questions 31 to 40. Now listen carefully and answer questions 31 to 40.

Hello everyone. I recently completed some important research into the way office space is designed and its impact on employees. The open plan office had been the norm for offices around the world. I'm sure you can picture one. A big room with maybe 50 or 100 workers sitting at their workstations. You might think open plan designs are a recent phenomenon, but they actually came about in the early 20th century, when 31businesses needed large numbers of people to do typing(31: typing). These employees, usually women, used to have to sit in rows working away at their typewriters under the watchful eye of their supervisor.

Over the years, designers have come up with several variations on the open theme. In particular, this has been achieved through furnishings. 32The early designs featured separate desks.(32: desks) A subsequent design introduced the concept of the cubicle. These can have either 33high screens that workers can't see over or a screen just low enough to keep a lookout for the manager(33: screens). Nowadays, with the increase in large call centres, a phenomenon known as hot desking is common. In this system, employees working on different shifts are asked to 34share the same workstation(34: share). As you can imagine, this is not very popular with staff as there is little opportunity to personalise your workspace.

In fact, my research shows that employees in general dislike the open plan design. As a part of my research, I conducted a survey of the staff in several large companies with open plan offices. The employees all voiced similar concerns about the design. Firstly, they felt that 35the open plan took away their privacy(35: privacy), but many also felt the need to carry their valuables with them at all times. So, to some extent, they also felt there was 36less security with these designs(36: security). Another common complaint may be something for employers to consider when choosing this type of design, and this was that these types of office space are 37bad for the health(37: health). I was intrigued by this possibility, but looking at staff records, it does seem to be borne out.

So, if this design is so unpopular, why is it so commonplace? Well, the answer to that is in the benefits that the employers feel this type of design has. Of course, there is the obvious benefit that this design significantly reduces costs, both in terms of 38rent and even in terms of energy use(38: energy). Employers also argue that an open plan design leads to better teamwork and communication. They also feel that new staff can learn from observing other workers, so this design can actually 39help in training staff(39: training). However, the complaints voiced by employees seem to suggest that the disadvantages far outweigh the advantages. The reality is that unhappy workers are unlikely to work well together, and there is plenty of evidence to suggest workers are frequently 40distracted from their work by the noise that is all around them(40: noise). What is clear is that, as designers, we need to think carefully about the impact that our design has both on the staff involved and the work that they do. That is the end of Part 4.
