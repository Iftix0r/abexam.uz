The Pyramid of Cestius

A 2000-year-old pyramid in the city of Rome has been restored by archaeologists

Though Rome draws tourists from around the world to its many impressive sites, 1one notable monument there has never attracted nearly as much interest(FALSE — hech qachon ko'p qiziqish uyg'otmagan): The Pyramid of Cestius. But why would there be a pyramid in Italy? After the Roman conquest of Egypt in 30 B.C., Egyptian architectural style became the fashion in Rome. Though obelisks and other monuments inspired by Egypt's great pyramids sprung up around the city, only two actual pyramids are known to have been built. The only one left standing, the Pyramid of Cestius, was designed as the burial pyramid for a Roman politician named Caius Cestius, 2who ordered that the building work be completed within a period of 330 days(NOT GIVEN — faqat 330 kun muddat aytilgan, o'limi haqida ma'lumot yo'q).

Construction took place at some point between 18 B.C. and 12 B.C. Cestius' pyramid had a layer of white Carrara marble on the outside, and 8was constructed from brick held together by a basic kind of cement(brick — ichi g'ishtdan qurilgan) on the inside. One of the things that strikes you when you look at the pyramid is how steep it is, so that 9the shape of Cestius' pyramid is quite unlike that of typical Egyptian ones(shape — shakli Misr piramidalaridan farq qiladi). This is a difference that could have been the result of inaccurate information sent back to Rome by soldiers who saw the pyramids in person in Egypt. Alternatively, Roman builders could have drawn inspiration from the pyramids in Nubia, a region located in what is today northern Sudan and southern Egypt.

At the time of its construction, since there was a strict Roman law prohibiting the placement of tombs within the city itself, 10the Pyramid of Cestius would have stood in countryside(countryside — shahar tashqarisida (qishloqda) turgan). Rome grew enormously during the next two centuries, and, by the 3rd century A.D., the pyramid would have been surrounded by buildings. We also know that in the 3rd century A.D., the Pyramid of Cestius was hidden behind a high wall on the orders of Emperor Aurelian; this probably helped it survive throughout the centuries to come, even as other ancient monuments disappeared.

By the Middle Ages, the pyramid was covered in vegetation and thick dirt, and 3popular myth had developed that it might be a tomb for one of the twin brothers Romulus and Remus(TRUE — Rimni asos solgan aka-ukalardan birining qabri deb o'ylashgan), who were regarded as the men who had established the city of Rome. Cestius' actual tomb within the pyramid and the inscription identifying the pyramid as his weren't rediscovered until the 1660s when the pyramid underwent restoration. During excavations, when trees and plants were cleared away, two marble bases were found in front of the pyramid, as well as 11fragments of bronze statues(statues — bronza haykal parchalari) that had once stood on them on either side of the pyramid. The people employed to excavate the pyramid did not find the urn that would have contained Cestius' remains, 12but they did come across a tunnel(tunnel — tunnel — o'g'rilar kirgan bo'lishi mumkin). It was quite possible, therefore, that robbers had at some earlier time removed the contents of the tomb. But while some of the features of Cestius' tomb no longer exist, at least the pyramid itself has survived.

Today, the foundations of Cestius' pyramid rest below street level near an intersection with heavy traffic, so that 4passing tourists and residents could easily fail to notice its full height of 119 feet(FALSE — balandligini osongina sezmay qolishadi). Across the intersection is the Piramide station, located on Line B of the Rome Metro. In 2011, the Japanese clothing company entrepreneur Yuzo Yagi, president of Yagi Tsusho Ltd, announced his intention to help the Italian government pay for an ambitious renovation of the Pyramid of Cestius. 'It's an act of gratitude,' he later told journalists. '5Our company has grown thanks to Italy(NOT GIVEN — faqat minnatdorchilik aytilgan, me'morchilikka qiziqishi haqida yo'q).' Work began at the site shortly after Yagi signed an official agreement with the Special Superintendency for the Archaeological Heritage of Rome, and 6was completed ahead of schedule thanks to his 2-million-euro contribution(TRUE — muddatidan oldin tugagan).

As archaeologist Leonardo Guarnieri explained to journalists, officials are now conducting tours of the newly renovated pyramid twice a month by reservation. Visitors who take advantage of the tour can make their way through a narrow corridor in order to enter the burial chamber itself. It is within these walls that they can admire the frescoes: watercolour paintings typical of the time. In the chamber, it is possible to make out four frescoes of the winged Roman goddess of war, Victoria, a figure from Roman legend, as well as of a series of vases, the type that would have been used for special rituals and purification purposes. We know from the writings of earlier visitors that there used to be more here, 7but the majority have disappeared over time(FALSE — ko'pchiligi yo'qolib ketgan).

Only one problem remains now that the restoration is complete. The white exterior of the Pyramid of Cestius will have to be cleaned every few months 13to remove the layer of urban pollution(pollution — shahar ifloslanishi). A team of free-climbers will be employed to do the job, in order to avoid placing builder's scaffolding around the newly welcoming monument.

Flood control in the USA

A  Americans have always feared floods, and with good reason. Floods are the most common and costly of the large natural disturbances affecting the United States. Approximately 9 of every 10 presidential disaster declarations are associated with them and, 14after total insurance losses from a series of devastating floods between 1990 and 1997 reached nearly $34 billion(iv — The economic implications of flooding — toshqinlarning iqtisodiy oqibatlari), government policy changed. 20The USA has subsequently spent even more than that trying to control floods(insurance — sug'urta to'lovlaridan ham ko'p sarflangan) by building structures such as levees and dams to modify the ways rivers flow.

B  However, although we are well aware of the damage floods do, 15we have not, until recently, fully appreciated the many beneficial aspects of flooding(ii — Understanding the positive side of flooding — toshqinning foydali tomonlari). Floods are critical for maintaining and restoring many of the important services provided to humans by ecosystems. Among other things, 21regular flooding ensures high levels of diversity in the riverine environment(diversity — xilma-xillik) by providing a critical habitat for fish, waterfowl, animals, insects, and plants. 22Floodwaters also replenish agricultural land with key nutrients vital to crop production(nutrients — ozuqa moddalar), and 23transport the sediment that is necessary to maintain downstream delta and coastal land and prevent erosion(sediment — cho'kindi (sediment)). As our knowledge of floods has deepened, it has become apparent that floods present us with a paradox. On the one hand, we want to prevent them, because they threaten our lives and ways of life. On the other hand, we find ourselves searching for ways to allow or even reintroduce flooding, because it supports the biological infrastructure that makes valued aspects of our lives possible.

C  The focus of policy began to change dramatically in the wake of massive flooding in the upper Mississippi and lower Missouri river basins from June to August of 1993. 24Many of the flood defenses failed during this episode(defenses — himoya inshootlari ishlamagan). In response, the President ordered a special committee to investigate causes, explore how human actions might have exacerbated the situation, and determine what the nation should do to prevent a repeat event. Known as the Galloway Report, the committee's findings and recommendations 16marked the first time that the crucial ecological protections provided by wetlands and upland forests were explicitly acknowledged(i — Official recognition of the importance of natural flood protection — tabiiy himoya rasman tan olingan). This included their ability to store water, slow its entry into the river system, and absorb excess nutrients. The committee pointed out that losing these protections through changes in land use significantly increases runoff from farmland with serious consequences for water quality. Above all, the report acknowledged that floods are recurring phenomena that must be accepted and anticipated.

D  A common theme in the findings from analyzing a range of flood episodes is the importance of connections between riverine ecosystems, their floodplains, and the broader landscapes through which they flow. It is worth noting that efforts to prevent rivers from reclaiming their floodplains rarely pay off in the long run. On the contrary, most of the flood costs over the past decade have been associated with damage to structures in those floodplains. We have also learned that building on floodplains harms wetlands by reducing their ability to hold water and retain nitrogen, phosphorus, and other contaminants. 17Floodplains are the sole safeguard the environment has against these and other harmful effects of floods(viii — The only natural defence against flood damage — yagona tabiiy himoya — floodplains). The aftermath of flooding caused by storm Alberto in Georgia in 1994 highlighted the important role they play in minimizing destruction. When floodwaters eroded agricultural fields, picking up soil particles contaminated with herbicides, pesticides, and fungicides, the large, forested floodplains in the area filtered out those particles. They never entered rivers to cause harm.

E  Fortunately, although the mechanics of re-establishing floodplains are daunting, it is not impossible; 18their ecosystems are resilient(v — Nature's rapid powers of recovery — tabiat tez tiklanadi), as studies on recurrent floods in the lower Missouri River have shown. Annual spring floods have long been controlled in this portion of the river, which stretches from Sioux City, Iowa, to St. Louis, Missouri. As a result, it was almost totally disconnected from its floodplain and, by 1990, state or federal agencies had classified 16 fish species, 7 plant species, 6 insect species, 2 mussel species, 4 reptile species, 14 bird species, and 3 mammal species within the floodplain complex as endangered, threatened, or rare. But the situation began to change with the great Midwest floods of 1993, 1995, and 1996. Post-flood studies have demonstrated that plants, zooplankton, aquatic insects, fish, turtles, and other animals immediately exploited the reconnection with the floodplain.

F  Ensuring that the necessary changes in the landscape are actually made requires a good deal of thought, however. In particular, 19the fragmentation and lack of coordination that has characterized policy concerning floodplains(vi — Bureaucratic obstacles to natural flood controls — byurokratik to'siqlar) in the United States must be addressed. Decisions on developments such as construction that could affect floodplains are made primarily at county and municipal levels, yet 25the current policy structure gives the federal government the most responsibility for floodplain management(A — qarorlar federal darajada to'plangan). It provides little incentive and few mechanisms for the county and municipal governments to help. Nor do individual states have much opportunity to become involved, even though they may be best situated to coordinate the diverse actions and decisions that are needed. In the end, all such policy decisions and the accompanying management actions 26must be based on people weighing up the costs and benefits of different spending priorities(C — moliyaviy resurslarni taqsimlash bo'yicha tanlov). In other words, they are political. Scientists alone will not, and should not, select which options are appropriate. Rather, their contribution will be to spell out the implications and the trade-offs of different policy options and management scenarios.

How a prehistoric predator took to the skies

Is that a bird in the sky? A plane? No, it's a pterosaur. Kate Thomas meets Professor Matthew Wilkinson, who built a life-size model to find out how this prehistoric predator ever got off the ground

Pterosaurs existed from the Triassic period, 220 million years ago, to the end of the Cretaceous period, 65 million years ago, when South America pulled away from Africa and the South Atlantic was formed. 27They are among the least understood of all the extinct reptiles(D — reptiles — uchuvchi sudralib yuruvchilar (reptiles)) that once spent their lives in the skies while the dinosaurs dominated the land. Pterosaurs had no feathers, but 28at least part of their bodies was covered in hair(L — hair — tanasida jun (hair) bo'lgan), not unlike bats. Some believe this is an indication they were warm-blooded. Researchers also debate whether pterosaurs travelled on the ground 29by walking on their hind legs, like birds(F — hind legs — faqat orqa oyoqlarida), or by using all fours, relying on their three-toed front feet as well as their four-toed rear feet.

Pterosaurs were vertebrates, meaning they were the first species possessing backbones to become airborne, but scientists have never quite understood their flight technique. 30How, they wondered, did such a heavy creature ever manage to take off?(J — weight — og'irligiga qaramay uchgan) How could a wing that appears to have been 31supported by fine, hollow bones(I — strength — ingichka, ichi bo'sh suyaklar — kuchli emas) have carried one into the sky? Then came the discovery of a site in Brazil's Araripe basin. Here, not only were hundreds of fossils of amphibians* and other reptiles found, but archaeologists unearthed a number of very well-preserved pterosaurs. The anhanguera – 32a fish-eating sub-species of pterosaur(B — fish — baliq bilan oziqlangan) that ruled the skies in the Cretaceous period – was among them. With a wingspan of up to 12 metres, they would have made an amazing sight in the sky – had any human been there to witness it.

'I've been studying pterosaurs for about eight years now,' says Dr Matthew Wilkinson, a professor of zoology at Cambridge University. With an anhanguera fossil as his model, Wilkinson began gradually reconstructing its skeletal structure in his Cambridge studio. 33The probability of finding three-dimensional pterosaur fossils anywhere is slim(YES — bunday fosillar kam uchraydi). 'That was quite a find,' he says. 'Their bones are usually crushed to dust.' Once the structure was complete, it inspired him to make a robot version as a way to understand the animal's locomotion. With a team of model-makers, he has built a remote-controlled pterosaur in his studio. 'Fossils show just how large these creatures were. 34I've always been interested in how they managed to launch themselves(NO — maqsad — qanday havoga ko'tarilganini bilish, uzoq masofa emas), so I thought the real test would be to actually build one and fly it.'

Wilkinson hasn't been alone in his desire to recreate a prehistoric beast. Swiss scientists recently announced they had built an amphibious robot that could walk on land and swim in water using the sort of backbone movements that must have been employed by the first creatures to crawl from the sea. But Wilkinson had the added complication of working out his beast's flight technique. Unlike those of bats or flying squirrels, pterosaur wings – soft, stretchy membranes of skin tissue – are thought to have reached from the chest right to the ankle, reinforced by fibres that stiffened the wing and prevented tearing. Smaller subspecies flapped their wings during takeoff. That may have explained the creatures' flexibility, but it did not answer the most pressing question: 37how did such heavy animals manage to launch themselves into the sky?(D — og'ir pterozavrlar qanday havoga ko'tarilgan — asosiy savol) Working with researchers in London and Berlin, Wilkinson began to piece together the puzzle.

It emerged that the anhanguera had an elongated limb called the pteroid. It had previously been thought the pteroid pointed towards the shoulder of the creature and supported a soft forewing in front of the arm. But if that were the case, the forewing would have been too small and ineffectual for flight. However, 38to the surprise of many scientists, fossils from the Araripe basin showed the pteroid possibly faced the opposite way(A — kutilmagan tomonga qaragan), creating a much greater forewing that would have caught the air, working in the same way as the flaps on the wings of an aeroplane. So, with both feet on the ground, the anhanguera might have simply faced into the wind, spread its wings and risen up into the sky. Initial trials in wind tunnels proved the point – models of pterosaurs with forward-facing pteroids were not only adept at gliding, but were agile flyers in spite of their size. 'This high-lift capability would have significantly reduced the minimum flight speed, allowing even the largest forms to take off without difficulty,' Wilkinson says. 'It would have enabled them to glide very slowly and 35may have been instrumental in the evolution of large size by the pterosaurs(YES — pteroid katta o'lchamga sabab bo'lgan bo'lishi mumkin).'

Resting in the grass at the test site near Cambridge, the robot-model's wings ripple in the wind. In flight, the flexible membrane, while much stiffer than the real thing, allows for a smooth takeoff and landing. But the model has been troubled by other mechanical problems. 'Unlike an aircraft, which is stabilised by the tail wing at the back, 39the model is stabilised by its head, which means it can start spinning around(B — boshi bilan aylanib ketadi — boshqarib bo'lmaydi). That's the most problematic bit as far as we're concerned,' Wilkinson says. '36We've had to take it flying without the head so far(NOT GIVEN — boshi bilan muammo bor, lekin bir nechta nusxa haqida gap yo'q).' When it flies with its head attached, Wilkinson will finally have proved his point.

So what's next for the zoologist – perhaps a full-size Tyrannosaurus rex? 'No,' he tells me: 'We're desperate to build really big pterosaurs. I'm talking creatures with even greater wingspans, weighing a quarter of a ton. But,' he adds, just as one begins to fear for the safety and stress levels of pilots landing nearby at Cambridge City Airport, 'it's more likely we'll start off with one of the smaller, flapping pterosaurs.' This is certainly more reassuring. 40Let's hope he is content to leave it at that(C — kichik modellar qurish istagi bilan cheklansin).

* amphibians: animals that can live both in water and on land
