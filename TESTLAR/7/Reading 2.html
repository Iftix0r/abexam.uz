Thomas Cole: American Nature Painter

Thomas Cole (1801–1848) was America’s first major landscape artist. Though he was born in England and spent his formative time in Europe, his identity was as an American nature painter. He was also an advocate and protector of untouched American land threatened by industrial development.

Cole’s father, a small-time businessman, moved his family from place to place in northwestern England. In the process, Cole worked as a print shop apprentice, designing styles of cloth and later 1making reproductions of well-known paintings(TRUE — mashhur rasmlar nusxasi). In 1818, the family sailed to the United States, settling at first in the east coast city of Philadelphia. 2The teenage Cole stayed behind on the east coast as his parents traveled westward(FALSE — g'arbga ota-onasi ketdi, Cole qoldi). From this point, his career as a largely self-taught artist began.

3Cole began to travel and wherever he went — including, briefly, to the Caribbean — he sketched what he saw(NOT GIVEN — Karib orollarida sotgani aytilmagan). He had little patience with cities and distanced himself from them when he could. At the Pennsylvania Academy of the Fine Arts, he became familiar with new American and European work. But within a few years, he felt ready to head to New York City, where he 4made lasting contacts in the still small art world(TRUE — lasting contacts = uzoq muddatli aloqalar), and 5was able to sell some paintings(NOT GIVEN — nimani tasvirlagani aytilmagan). And on an 1825 trip up the Hudson River, he found the subject that he would make his own: the American landscape, which he approached as both a mirror of reality and an object of fantasy.

Encouraged, Cole resolved to leave for Europe, a move that would let him see firsthand the art he had known mostly in reproduction. In terms of sales, London, the first stop, was a failure. No one noticed him, but he noticed a lot. He saw England suffering from chaos, with old social structures breaking down as a new industrial elite threatened a traditional wealthy rural class. And he saw artists responding to that. 6From England he went on to Italy, a more pleasant stay(NOT GIVEN — qayerda uzoqroq qolgani aytilmagan), though it was also suffering its own social issues. Outside Rome, he saw and painted countryside bathed in the hazy sunset light that he had seen and loved in 17th-century landscapes.

When Cole returned to America in 1832 he found it confusing and depressing. Though he had worked hard to leave his poor childhood behind through identification with a cultural elite, Cole found a new America: competitive, commercial, and uncivil — a land of uncontrolled and destructive greed. He was committed to the USA — he became a citizen in 1834 — but 7left New York City with his wife and children for the countryside(FALSE — oilasi ham qishloqqa ko'chgan).

What most disturbed him was the government’s seizure and destruction of natural forest lands. From his house he could see forests being rapidly cut down. Increasingly, he used painting to protest against these developments. Cole chose wilderness, and used the rest of his life to paint it.

One of Cole’s earliest pieces illustrating his dedication to the preservation of nature is The Tree, dated 1823. Done while he was living in the Caribbean, it’s an ink drawing of a single tree. But it is no ordinary tree: although realistically drawn, it looks bizarre, 8almost resembling some kind of animal(animal), swinging its arm-like branches and walking on its roots.

Cole’s paintings from his Italian trip feature a different kind of countryside. These landscapes focused not on pure and untouched nature, but on the 9ruins of a long-dead culture(ruins). One of his most famous works is a series called The Course of Empire (1834–36). In five separate paintings, it narrates the 10stages that an unnamed fictional civilization passes through(stages): from forest origins, to rural innocence, to proud flourishing high society, to destruction, to vanishing.

Returning to America, Cole settled on producing and selling landscape paintings. An early landscape, View of the Round-Top (1827), depicts thin clouds in the cool fresh air, 11a forested mountain(mountain), and a river winding in the distance. Paintings such as these were popular and sold quickly for high prices to New York’s new wealthy elite.

A similar image that was far more effective is the single, large 1836 painting titled View From Mount Holyoke (The Oxbow). From a bird’s-eye height we look down at the spot where the Connecticut River, flowing around an island-like mass, creates a question-mark shape known as the Oxbow. The painting divides vertically into two distinct halves. To the left is a wild cluster of old trees and dense vegetation, which looks messy and threatening, but full of life; to the right, flat, treeless, 12square-cut fields which are cultivated and fertile(fields), but feel bare and bland. In the foreground is a tiny self-portrait of Cole at work. 13He turns away from his canvas(canvas) and looks right at us, as if to say: Here are the alternatives that you will face; you choose.

When he died suddenly at 47, in 1848, he was widely mourned. After Cole’s death, respect for his life and work was shown by fellow artists in the form of paintings done in a similar style for just a few years. The most famous was Asher Brown Durand’s 1849 Kindred Spirits, which illustrates Cole and the nature-poet William Bryant engaged in deep discussion in a rugged paradise of New York State forest. Yet, within a few years, Cole was all but forgotten, and it took more than a century before he was rediscovered.

Growing more for less

Satellite technology is helping farmers boost crop yields

AFor farmers, working out the optimal amount of seed, fertiliser, pesticide and water to scatter on a field can make, or break, the subsequent harvest. Regular laboratory analyses of soil and plant samples from various parts of the field can help but such expertise is costly, and often unavailable. However, a new and cheaper method of doing this analysis is now on offer. Precise prescriptions for growing crops can be obtained quickly, and less expensively, 23by calculating the amount of electromagnetic radiation reflected from agricultural land. The data is collected by orbiting satellites(electromagnetic radiation).

BExamining the wavelength of the radiation that is reflected can reveal, with surprising precision, the properties of the soil, the quantity of crop being grown, and the levels in those crops of chlorophyll*, various minerals, moisture and other indicators of their quality. If recent and forecast weather data is added to the mix, detailed maps can be produced indicating exactly how, where and when crops should be grown. 20The service usually costs less than US $15 per hectare for a handful of readings a year, and can increase yields by as much as 10%(B — arzon va samarali).

CSuch precision farming using satellite-based intelligence is a relatively new technique. Even so, it is catching on quickly. 14Five times a year, for example, a French cereal-growers’ co-operative called Sevépi purchases satellite data and makes it available to its members in the form of maps of their fields(C — Fransiya fermerlari), divided into three or four colour-coded zones per hectare. For each zone, one of about 50 fertiliser formulas is recommended. On top of this, if the wheat in the field has already grown quite high early in the season, and heavy showers are expected, an appropriate dose of growth regulator is recommended for each zone. (Long, fragile stems break more easily in downpours.) Then, farm vehicles equipped with global-positioning system locators automatically mix and apply the prescribed dose to each area.

D18France is the pioneer in this sort of surveillance(D — yetakchi mamlakat). More farmland is analysed by satellite there than in any other country, according to 21Infoterra (a subsidiary of EADS Astrium), the firm that is France’s largest provider of such information(B — Infoterra ma'lumotni sun'iy yo'ldoshdan oladi), supplying data to companies such as Sevépi. Moreover, 24Henri Douche, head of Infoterra’s agriculture sales in Toulouse, reckons(Henri Douche) 15the amount of monitored farmland will increase as weather patterns change and farmers can no longer rely on the past as a guide to the future(D — ob-havo o'zgarishi). When confounded by the yield variations that these new weather patterns will bring, even farmers who are afraid of new technology will sign up, he says.

EInexpensive data on the productivity of land is advantageous to governments, too. Areas where fertilisers and pesticides are being applied excessively can be pinpointed, studied and regulated by environmental and land-use agencies. Guy Lafond, an agronomist with Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada, a government agency, says the satellite data it purchases is proving useful for the study of fields with declining productivity in the province of Saskatchewan. 16Overkill with nitrate fertilisers (which are also a source of greenhouse gases) appears partly responsible(E — ortiqcha o'g'it). And according to RapidEye, a German satellite operator, insurance companies are also studying satellite data with a view to 25selling insurance policies to governments of famine-prone countries that might be threatened by crop failure(crop failure).

FIn March, 22RapidEye began selling data that helps forecast harvests(D — RapidEye o'z sun'iy yo'ldoshlariga ega). ‘Too often, farmers limit productivity by managing fields uniformly,’ says Fredrick Jung-Rothenhausler, head of product development at the firm’s headquarters in Brandenburg an der Havel. ‘Our satellites are the first commercial satellites to include the Red-Edge band of the light spectrum, which is sensitive to changes in chlorophyll content. 19More research will be necessary to realise the full potential of the Red-Edge band(F — qo'shimcha tadqiqot kerak). However, this band can assist in monitoring vegetation health, improving species separation and also help in measuring protein and nitrogen content in biomass. The company’s data, which comes from both Europe and the Americas, breaks field productivity down into patches just five metres square.

GThe advantages that satellite technology provides in terms of precision farming do not have to be restricted to rich countries. In Africa, 26where many soils have become badly depleted of nutrients(nutrients), better fertiliser management would greatly improve the situation. As a consequence, the charitable trust World Agroforestry Centre, in the city of Nairobi, in Kenya, has begun to build up a catalogue of the radiation patterns derived from around 100,000 samples of African soils. The information is the key to understanding the potential of these soils to be more agriculturally productive. 17Once passed on to the International Centre for Tropical Agriculture, based in Colombia, South America, it is intended that the information be used to build a database called the ‘Digital Soil Map’(G — mamlakatlar o'rtasida ulashiladi). When ready, this will provide farmers with free forecasts, developed with regularly updated satellite imagery, across farmland in a number of countries in Africa. This is information which will almost certainly assist in improving crop yields. For a hunger-ravaged continent, that is good news indeed.

* chlorophyll: the green substance in plants that allows them to absorb sunlight

Rethinking the Past

It is by now a truism that the story of human evolution is being rethought. Discoveries have come thick and fast over the last decade or so, and these have forced us to rethink many crucial points, such as how old our species is - about 300,000 years old as opposed to 200,000 - and what extinct hominins, such as our cousins the Neanderthals, were really like. But because there are so many species and eras involved, it's hard to discern the common threads linking them.

However, I do think it's possible to draw out some overall messages from the blizzard of archaeological finds in recent years. 27Two things stand out to me. One is the growing evidence that many supposedly 'advanced' behaviours, such as architecture and art, can be traced much further back in time than we thought(A — tushunchamizdagi asosiy o'zgarishlar), often to hominin species that existed before modern humans. And the other is that we have badly misunderstood gender roles in prehistoric societies, imposing patriarchal values onto cultures that had very different ideas about how women should behave.

Let's start with architecture. At Kalambo Falls in Zambia, researchers found buried logs that had been shaped with stone tools so that they interlocked. They seem to have once been part of a larger structure, perhaps a building. 31This would be unsurprising if they weren't 476,000 years old. That's almost 200,000 years before our species, Homo sapiens, evolved(E — boshqa tur ham murakkab ish qila olgan). Extinct hominins also managed to settle in extreme places. For instance, 32we now know that extinct hominins such as the Denisovans lived on the frozen heights of high-altitude regions 200,000 years ago(F — og'ir sharoitda yashagan) - upending the old notion that such environments were only settled by modern humans around 3,600 years ago.

Art also seems to have been invented by older hominins. We have had evidence for a long time now that Neanderthals painted on cave walls. 35Even earlier species, such as Homo erectus, may also have made art(NO — neandertallar birinchi emas), for example by 36engraving patterns on shells(NOT GIVEN — qanchalik kam uchrashi aytilmagan). By far the most contentious claim in this area is that Homo naledi made art. H. naledi lived around 250,000 years ago, making it a contemporary of our species. However, it had quite a small brain, typical of older hominins - and was therefore, according to palaeoanthropological dogma, incapable of complex behaviours. Nevertheless, in the Rising Star cave system in South Africa where the H. naledi remains were found, 37researchers have found what seem to be etchings(NOT GIVEN — tadqiqot usullari haqida gap yo'q) - resembling rudimentary artwork - on the cave walls, 38though these have yet to be firmly dated(YES — yoshi aniq emas).

To say these claims about H. naledi are controversial is to understate the situation. Many experts say the evidence presented so far is completely inadequate to support them. 39The dispute has only been heightened by the way the results were released, in a non-traditional journal that publishes peer reviews publicly alongside the paper(YES — e'lon qilish usuli bahsni kuchaytirdi). My views on the H. naledi controversy are complicated. I do think more evidence is needed: in particular with regard to the dating of the etchings. At the same time, 40I think the species' small brains are a distraction(NO — miya hajmi asosiy omil emas). Palaeoanthropologists got fixated on brain size because it was what they could see: if what you have is skeletons, then all you know about brains are their shapes and sizes. But other properties, such as the brain's internal wiring, are surely equally important and may explain how a species like H. naledi might have been capable of complex behaviours, despite their small brains.

In a sense, we shouldn't be surprised that so many of these behaviours had their origins in older, extinct hominins. 28Evolution usually works by incremental steps and so does technology. The first birds weren't great at flying, and the first mobile phones weren't great at, well, anything really(A — rivojlanish asta-sekin). The idea that there was a sudden explosion of intelligence and creativity at some point in our evolution isn't inherently ridiculous: sometimes a system hits a tipping point and undergoes runaway change. But there was never that much evidence that human evolution worked this way. Instead, it seems the Neanderthals and many others all walked so we could run.

One way or another, the H. naledi story is going to be an example of letting our preconceptions get in the way of the evidence. The same is true for our ideas about gender in prehistory. 29Archaeology was invented by individuals with now unfashionably patriarchal views about gender, and those notions fed into their research. Today's researchers are trying to unpick this stuff(D — eskirgan qarashlar ta'siridagi tadqiqotlarni qayta baholash), and there have been some significant steps in recent years.

Perhaps the most dramatic was the demolition of 'Man the Hunter'. This was the idea, promoted for decades, that in most prehistoric societies the men went out to hunt and the women looked after the home. However, 33a meta-analysis published in June 2023 compiled data on several dozen foraging societies and found women hunted in 80 per cent of them(D — ovni kim qilgani haqida xato). In line with this, it emerged that an ancient spear-throwing tool called an atlatl enables women to launch projectiles at the same speed as men. We have also seen growing evidence of women occupying positions of authority in ancient societies. The Viking queen Thyra may have helped unify Denmark in the 900s. Going further back, 34an Iberian leader from around 4000 years ago turned out to be female, not male as many had assumed(B — hokimiyat kimda bo'lgani haqida xato), when proteins in her teeth were analysed.

It seems that the more we find out about past societies, the more our preconceptions about the ways society 'has to be' turn out to be wrong. Inequality, authoritarianism and patriarchy aren't inevitable. 30They're choices, and prehistory shows us that we can choose differently(A — adolatliroq jamiyat qurish mumkin).
