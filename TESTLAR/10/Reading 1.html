Transport and transport systems during the Roman Empire

At the height of the Roman Empire in the 2nd century AD, it was not uncommon for Romans to travel long distances by road across Europe and beyond. In fact, it was actually quite easy to travel, thanks to the empire's highly developed road and shipping networks. It has been calculated that Roman roads covered a total distance of over 400,000 kilometres, with more than 120,000 kilometres of this being of the type known as 'public roads' and 1a second system classified as 'military roads', both constructed by soldiers(TRUE — ikkala yo'l tizimini ham askarlar qurgan). The roads spread across the empire from Britain in the north to Morocco in the south, from Portugal in the west to Iraq in the east. Roads played a crucial role in the maintenance of the empire, allowing people and goods to move relatively swiftly. Tolls were charged for the use of many major roads, with stations where these tolls were paid often situated at bridges or at city gates.

Having the elaborate road network also meant that Roman troops could be sent rapidly to various parts of the empire in the event of emergencies, such as internal revolts or external threats. Therefore, 2the existence of these roads reduced the number of large and costly garrisons(FALSE — yo'llar garnizonlar sonini kamaytirgan, ko'paytirmagan)* needed throughout the empire.

Ordinary Romans would travel in a raeda, a carriage with four wooden wheels covered with iron and pulled by up to four horses. 3The raeda was the equivalent of the bus today(NOT GIVEN — raeda qanday masofaga yurgani haqida ma'lumot yo'q). It had wooden benches inside for passengers, and a cloth top or sometimes no top at all. Rich Romans travelled in a carpentum, which was essentially a luxury vehicle for the wealthy. The carpentum had four wheels, a wooden arched rooftop and seats. It was pulled by many horses and was 4built incorporating a form of suspension to make the ride less bumpy and disagreeable(TRUE — yo'lovchilar qulayligi uchun amortizator qo'yilgan). Romans also had a vehicle, called a plaustrum, that did not normally carry passengers – what would be considered a truck today. The plaustrum, constructed with a flat wooden base over four thick wheels and drawn by two oxen, was designed to carry heavy loads. It was very slow and could cover only 15 to 25 kilometres per day.

The fastest way to travel over heavily trafficked routes was by the cursus publicus. This was like a state-run postal service, but was also used to transport government or military officials. 5A certificate issued by the emperor was required in order to use the service(TRUE — imperator bergan guvohnoma kerak bo'lgan). A series of stations with fresh horses were built at short regular intervals of approximately 12 kilometres along the major road systems. It has been estimated that a typical trip by relays of horses was made at a rate of 66 to 103 kilometres per day.

Because of their iron-shod wheels, Roman vehicles made a lot of noise and were therefore forbidden by law from entering the vicinity of big Roman cities during the day. Fortunately, 6many roads had stations, called mansiones(NOT GIVEN — faqat oraliq masofa aytilgan, yo'llar kesishgan joy haqida gap yo'q), where Roman travellers could stop and break their journey, the equivalent of motorway rest areas today. These sometimes included inns where tired travellers could drink, eat and sleep. 7The stations were built by the government(FALSE — mansiones'ni xususiy tadbirkorlar emas, hukumat qurgan) at regular intervals of 25 to 30 kilometres. One of the factors that meant such roads could be safely used for trade was the fact that they were patrolled by the Roman army. Merchants knew that they would usually be protected from bandits and highwaymen.

It was not uncommon for well-to-do Romans to travel as tourists to places relatively far away such as Greece and Egypt. For this, they would often have to sail across the Mediterranean Sea, but as there were no regular passenger services, they travelled on ships which were transporting goods. Tourists had to find a ship, get the captain's approval and 8negotiate a price with him(price — kapitan bilan narx kelishilgan). Fortunately, there were many merchant ships going back and forth 9regularly along established routes(routes — kemalar belgilangan yo'nalishlarda qatnagan) in the Mediterranean so tourists had little difficulty finding a ship going to their chosen destination. Most Roman tourists would 10travel on the open deck of the ship(deck — ko'pchilik ochiq palubada sayohat qilgan), sometimes with hundreds of people crowded together. Food was not included in the price of transport, so passengers had to supply their own. Certain merchant ships 11provided cabins at the rear that could accommodate the richer passengers(cabins — boylar uchun kayutalar). Meanwhile, the wealthiest Romans had their own ships and 12employed a crew to sail them(crew — eng boylar o'z kemasi uchun ekipaj yollagan), much like on big private yachts today.

Travelling by ship in Roman times was relatively fast. For example, going from Brindisium in Italy to Patrae in Greece would take about three days. 13Commercial navigation was suspended during particularly bad storms(storms — kuchli bo'ronlarda qatnov to'xtatilgan), when the Mediterranean was too rough and dangerous for ships to sail.

Healthy buildings, productive people

Indoor building can influence our thinking, behavior and health

A  Have you felt tired at your desk lately? According to new research, some often overlooked environmental factors within buildings – the degree or type of ventilation, airborne contaminants, lighting and noise levels, for example – can play a surprisingly large role in how good or bad you feel, and even how well you think. It is estimated that we spend more than 90 percent of our time inside, but 14builders in general have paid little attention to the health aspects of indoor spaces(iv — The low priority given to making buildings healthy for users — quruvchilar binolarning sog'liq jihatlariga kam e'tibor bergan), instead focusing on superficial design features and on meeting minimum environmental standards to keep costs down.

B  Researchers at Harvard University in the US looked at the effects of indoor air quality on workers' cognitive performance – important both for health and for companies' bottom lines, says Joseph Allen, assistant professor of environmental health and principal investigator of the studies. 'The true cost of running our buildings should take into account the health and productivity of people in those buildings,' Allen says. In fact, an analysis by the team showed that the cost of making alterations that could boost cognitive performance significantly was minuscule compared with the related increase in productivity – 15about $40 per person, per year, compared with a $6,500 increase in employee productivity(viii — The financial benefits of efficient workplaces — kichik xarajat evaziga katta moliyaviy foyda).

C  The team's first study, published in Environmental Health Perspectives, was conducted at the Syracuse Center of Excellence, a US research organization 24devoted to studying and developing sustainability(sustainability — markaz barqarorlikni o'rganadi). The center houses a laboratory that allows researchers to test a wide range of indoor air quality factors using sophisticated ventilation. Each day, the indoor environment was controlled with a ventilation system which included small amounts of carbon dioxide and volatile organic compounds, or VOCs – the toxic by-products of office supplies commonly found in the workplace. The office workers were blinded to the conditions. For six days over two weeks, 1624 office workers with administrative, technical, professional or managerial jobs came to the lab(vi — Workers were assessed outside of their usual workplace — xodimlar odatiy ish joyida emas, laboratoriyada sinovdan o'tgan) and 25performed their regular work under minimal supervision(supervision — minimal nazorat ostida ishlashgan), as well as participating in an hour and a half of cognitive testing at the end of the day. The cognitive tasks used in the study had participants respond to online scenarios that mimic real-life situations, 26such as running a city as a mayor(mayor — shahar meri rolini o'ynash). The findings are remarkable for showing in a well-controlled study, that 23indoor air quality can significantly lower cognitive performance(B — Craig Zimring — Zimring: ichki havo sifati kognitiv qobiliyatni sezilarli pasaytiradi) in buildings that would otherwise meet current building standards, notes environmental psychologist Craig Zimring, director of Georgia Tech University's SimTigrate Design Lab in the US. 20'If these findings are replicated in other labs and in real-world work environments,' he says(B — Craig Zimring — Zimring: natijalar takrorlansa, sog'liq va samaradorlik yaxshilanadi), 'they could lead to designs that provide much more outside air through mechanical ventilation or operable windows, for example' – in turn, promoting better health and performance.

D  In a second study, reported in Building and Environment, the Harvard-Syracuse team moved from the lab to the real world. Using the same methodology, they compared test scores of 109 people working in 10 buildings – about 12 people per site – 17in two buildings in each of five cities(iii — The assessment of workplaces in several locations — beshta shahardagi binolar baholangan). When the team examined the buildings for differences, they found that certified buildings had lower humidity levels and brighter light than the non-certified buildings. That said, the differences in humidity and lighting didn't entirely explain the differing cognitive function scores of people on the job. This has led researchers to believe that other factors, such as 21how someone feels about their working environment, may also play a part(C — Piers MacNaughton — MacNaughton: ish joyiga munosabat ham rol o'ynaydi), notes Harvard research fellow Piers MacNaughton, who led the study.

E  A recently constructed building on the Washington University campus in St. Louis, US, 18is providing another opportunity to examine how green building design can affect health and behavior(i — The effects of a new environmentally friendly building on the people who work there — yangi 'yashil' binoning u yerda ishlovchilarga ta'siri). Hillman Hall was designed with the highest environmental and energy standards available, as well as design elements intended to foster more collaboration and physical activity, says Amy Eyler, associate professor and assistant dean for public health at the Brown School at Washington University. The design includes easy access to stairways, plenty of natural light, generous spaces for collaboration, desks that allow people to sit or stand up, and inviting places to walk. Unlike traditional hallways designed as long corridors with private offices to either side, for example, these hallways include classrooms, offices and spaces to sit, so they are multi-use – 'you have many reasons to walk them,' says Eyler. To test how the building might affect people's behavior, Eyler conducted a pre-test before people moved in, and a post-test one year later. Her team collected data on the three main features the designers were attempting to foster: physical activity, collaboration and sustainable practices. Results showed that 22people were more likely to take part in spontaneous collaborations(D — Amy Eyler — Eyler: yangi binoda hamkorlik ko'paygan) in the new space than in the older ones, and to be physically active.

F  Like other researchers in this growing field, 19Eyler sees multidisciplinary collaboration as the key to success(v — Achieving design efficiency by involving a range of specialists — turli sohadagi mutaxassislar hamkorligi). Administrators, public health faculty and architects worked together to create a building that captured the university's vision for a space that fosters collegial work and health for faculty, staff and students – a shared goal evidenced by the fact that the architecture firm that designed the building also funded part of the research. 'Complex projects like this one need teams of people with varied backgrounds and expertise in order to solve them,' Eyler says.

All in the family

A person's brothers and sisters can have a powerful effect on their adult behaviour

We can choose our friends, the saying goes, but we cannot choose our family of origin. Our parents provide the genetic material and make powerful early role models, but 27even more influential in determining what kind of adult we will become are our brothers and sisters(NO — aka-uka/opa-singillar ota-onadan ham ko'proq ta'sir qiladi) – our siblings. They occupy a position of unique intimacy in our lives and cast a longer shadow than many recognise or are prepared to acknowledge. By turns enraging and lovable, familiar and mysterious, 28our brothers and sisters are the human beings who people our first social relationships(NOT GIVEN — jins haqida hech narsa aytilmagan). And a sibling relationship is the most enduring relationship many of us will ever have – 29no less emotionally intense than the bonds we form with our spouses and our own children(NOT GIVEN — faqat taqqoslangan, turmushdan keyin e'tibor kamayishi aytilmagan) – if only because they started when we were so young.

Surprising new research by Dr Gene Brody found that having older children who do well in school and are well liked by other children leads to parental 'basking' – increasing mothers' self-esteem. It is clear that in turn 30this is associated with more positive parenting of younger children, who display fewer behavioural problems as a result(YES — kichik farzandlarga ijobiy ta'sir). Conversely, parents who get a difficult first child may in turn experience a negative spiral of household tension.

International sports star Piri Weepu is a rugby hard man, but at least some of his tough temperament was forged long ago in the family home. His older brother Billy started rugby-tackling little Piri when he was just four – to 'harden him up'. It seemed to work: Piri followed his brother in playing for the under-sevens league before he even started school. There are as many such stories as there are families: in the formative hothouse of the family, siblings interact in complex ways with the powerful forcefields of parents, genetics and personality already at work. It starts with birth order, which determines play roles: the firstborn leader, the middle-child mediator and the rebellious youngest. 31These are roles that can stick for life(B — tug'ilish tartibi butun umr saqlanadigan rollarni belgilaydi).

Clinical psychologist Claire Cartwright says she has clients who believe, 32and it might not have been true(C — mijozlarning tasavvuri noto'g'ri bo'lishi mumkin), that they were treated differently from their siblings, that one child in the family was preferred over others. They are quite convinced that the parental judgements on them all those years ago were harsher, and that they were the ones who always got into trouble. Later, in the workplace, such a person might be particularly defensive, and so behave in a way more prone to attracting harsh judgements.

However, the research shows that being Mummy or Daddy's favourite has its own traps. If you were the easy-going, co-operative child at home, you probably excelled at pleasing parents and, later, bosses. But you might, as an adult, find it hard to be assertive with authority figures and lack direction. On the other hand, the sibling who is disruptive and annoying as a child may be able to reinterpret that role later, 33turning those attention-seeking characteristics into strengths like determination and leadership(C — salbiy xususiyatlar kattalikda kuchli tomonga aylanadi).

How is it that full siblings, despite sharing DNA, can turn out so differently? One answer is that, in fact, each sibling grows up in a different family, a unique micro-culture. For example, the firstborn is, for a while, an only child, and therefore has a completely different experience of the parents than those born later. The parents themselves are growing up too, weathering hardship or good fortune, 34so one sibling might experience stability and closeness while another might be raised in the midst of crisis(D — oila muhiti vaqt o'tishi bilan o'zgaradi).

Of course, there are many positive aspects of sibling relationships. Annette Henderson, a lecturer in psychology, says firstborn children learn vocabulary more quickly than their siblings because they are not competing to spend one-on-one time with parents. But 35younger children in turn benefit from unintentional instruction from their bigger brothers and sisters(G — learning experiences — kattalardan beixtiyor o'rganish), acquiring entire phrases and an understanding of social concepts such as politeness. Similarly, a Cambridge University study of 140 children found that even when there is a great deal of conflict, 36siblings will still create a rich world of play and make-believe(B — imaginative games — xayoliy o'yinlar) that extends them developmentally. Love-hate relationships were common among the children, but even those who fought the most had as many positive interactions as the other sibling pairs. It is also true that children compete for parental attention by making themselves different from their brothers and sisters, particularly if they are close in age. A 2003 research paper studied adolescents from 185 families over two years, finding that those who changed, to differentiate themselves from their siblings, increased the amount of warmth they gained from parents, and also 37developed stronger personalities and a better sense of their own identity(F — greater individuality — kuchliroq shaxsiyat, o'ziga xoslik).

Another consideration is that many families today are smaller and more intimate than their historical counterparts. This may be advantageous because 38siblings tend to know each other better and remain lifelong friends(I — closer relationships — umrbod do'st bo'lib qolishadi) compared to children from bigger or more widely spaced families, who perhaps lose touch with one another after leaving home.

Then there was a 2010 American study which found that having a sister, whether younger or older, meant that 10- to 14-year-olds 39were less likely to feel lonely, unloved, self-conscious or fearful(K — mental well-being — yolg'izlik va qo'rquv kamroq — ruhiy salomatlik). The same study found that having a loving sibling of either gender 40promoted good deeds, such as helping a neighbour around the house(D — generous behaviour — yaxshi ishlar — saxovatli xulq) or helping other children at school.
