A look at the history of poetry

Unlike other literary forms that we can date to precise texts and time periods, it is a challenge to pinpoint the earliest work of poetry. In one form or another, poetry has been around for thousands of years. Indeed, poetry as an art form may even predate literacy. Many ancient works, such as the Vedas (1700 – 1200 BCE), were composed in poetic form to aid memorization and oral transmission of information in prehistoric and ancient societies. 1Poetry appears among the earliest records of most literate cultures(TRUE — deyarli barcha savodli madaniyatlarda she'riyat bor), with poetic fragments found engraved on ancient stones.

The lengthy adventure poem known as an epic might be considered as the first type of poetry. The Epic of Gilgamesh is often cited as one of the earliest epic works, dating back to the 18th century BCE. Consisting of poems written in an ancient language, it’s a text that was discovered through many different versions during archaeological excavations. Other examples of early epic poems include the Mahabharata and the Ramayana, 2the latter of which has become an important narrative in both Hindu and Buddhist mythology throughout regions of Asia(FALSE — Osiyoda muhim asar).

A list of the most notable works of epic poetry – at least in the Western world – would have to include the Iliad and the Odyssey, both works of Greek mythology that have been attributed by academics to the Greek poet Homer. The Iliad recounts the battle between Achilles, a famed warrior, and King Agamemnon during the Trojan War. 3A companion of sorts to the Iliad, the Odyssey depicts the long journey of its hero(NOT GIVEN — qachon topilgani aytilmagan), Odysseus, after the conflict is over.

Sonnets* were a form of poetry that came into being in the 13th century. The rhyme scheme of a sonnet varies depending upon whether it is Italian or English. 4The Italian poet Petrarca, after whom the Petrarchan sonnet is named(TRUE — Petrarchan sonnet), is perhaps one of the most well-known early writers of the sonnet, in which he specialized. Following his work in the 13th century, other poets created variations of the sonnet, but 5it became widely known as an English poetic form through the work of William Shakespeare in the 16th century(NOT GIVEN — sifati baholanmagan). However, its popularity declined in the Restoration period (1660 – 1689).

The Restoration Period in England also saw the rise of literary elites, such as John Dryden. Perhaps one of the best-known names of English literary criticism, translation, drama and poetry, Dryden made his name as a poet 6with his poems making fun of important figures or genres(TRUE — kulgili she'rlar). The form of these mock-heroic poems, and the use of poetic form for satire, did not end with Dryden. Alexander Pope carried on Dryden’s tradition of using poetry for comic purposes.

The 19th century saw the emergence of many poets of importance. 7The so-called English Romantics included figures like William Blake(Romantics), Samuel Taylor Coleridge, William Wordsworth and John Keats. During the same period, 8American poetry produced what are known as the fireside poets(fireside). Writers like Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, Oliver Wendell Holmes, and William Cullen Bryant belonged to this group. In other circles, Walt Whitman revolutionized the 19th-century American spirit with his 'Leaves of Grass', while most of Emily Dickinson’s unconventional poems, written in the mid-to-late 1800s, were published only after her death.

During the 20th century, the waves of change brought about by world events influenced poetry, resulting in works by poets with distinct voices who came to enjoy global circulation. Around the turn of the 20th century, 9Whitman continued to edit and alter his 1855 edition of 'Leaves of Grass', which introduced a new period(Whitman) – and a variety of forms – for poetry. The extraordinary modernist style of Gertrude Stein overwhelmed American readers who bought her work 'Tender Buttons' in 1914. Those same readers were to be surprised again a short time later by 10T.S. Eliot’s employment of ancient languages(languages) and obscure references to other poetical texts in his famous poem, 'The Waste Land', written in 1922.

Another example of modernist poetry is the hugely important work of Claude McKay. 11An African-American poet born in Jamaica(Jamaica) who emigrated to the US in 1912, he carried his homeland into his distinctly American poetic voice. Writing on war, racism and memories connected with his birthplace, McKay wrote notable poems such as 'If We Must Die' and 'The Tropics in New York'.

The 20th century also witnessed a number of poets winning the Nobel Prize, from the United States to India. Rabindranath Tagore, who resisted pressure from the English language used by the rulers of the country at that time and 12wrote solely in his mother tongue, Bengali(Bengali), won this esteemed award in 1913. He was followed by 13Irish poets William Butler Yeats in 1923 and Seamus Heaney in 1995(Irish).

* sonnet: a poem of fourteen lines with a fixed rhyme scheme

The development of television and its introduction in Britain

AThe possibility of seeing events without being present at them was a dream of humanity for countless centuries. When the telephone emerged as a practical device in the late 1870s, the idea of also ‘seeing by electricity’ caught the public imagination, and as early as 1878 a cartoonist conceived of the ‘telephonoscope’, a wildly accurate prediction of global video communication. Various experiments were conducted, and two forms of television were invented almost simultaneously: the electronic and the mechanical.

BIn 1897 a German physicist, Ferdinand Braun, developed a method of displaying electronic traces on a fluorescent screen coated inside an evacuated glass bulb. He called this the cathode-ray oscilloscope. A few years later a British scientist, A. A. Campbell Swinton, realized that Braun’s device could be used to display pictures transmitted electronically. 20Campbell Swinton wrote about the possibility of a television system in a scientific journal(B — Braun ixtirosi uchun maqola yozgan), Nature, in 1911. 14However, at that time it could not be built, as the components were not available(B — qismlar hali mavjud emas edi).

CThe real pioneer of electronic television was Vladimir Zworykin. His interest in the field developed when he worked on a cathode-ray tube receiver in St. Petersburg. In 1918 he left Russia for the United States, and in 1923 he patented the principles of the Iconoscope, a camera tube, and in 1924 the Kinescope, or television receiver. The Radio Corporation of America (RCA) recognized the potential of Zworykin’s ideas, and 21in 1929 funded a laboratory for him to continue his research(E — RCA moliyalashtirgan). 17The work was conducted in secret(C — maxfiy tadqiqot) and Zworykin did not publish his paper describing the Iconoscope in detail until 1933. This was the breakthrough that made electronic television a practical possibility. For the first time, a pick-up device could televise studio and outdoor scenes at a standard of definition that would bear comparison with the cinema.

DMeanwhile, in Britain, public interest in television was stimulated by a Scotsman, John L. Baird. He loved experimenting and, in 1922, found the cause to which he was to devote his life—to invent mechanical television. As he lacked a real understanding of electronics, and would probably have been unaware of Zworykin’s work, he began his research using very simple mechanical equipment. Baird successfully transmitted his first crude pictures in April 1925, and gave the first public demonstration of television in 1926. But it was not until 1929 that the government accepted that the technical quality of his pictures had improved sufficiently to allow broadcasting. Transmissions began on frequencies available when normal radio programs had finished for the day. Baird used the late transmission time to his advantage—it became an opportunity to encourage performers from top London shows to the medium. 15They dropped into his nearby studio after finishing on stage and performed a song or a dance for one of his shows(D — artistlar spektakldan keyin kelishgan). 22He developed a large amount of material(A — texnika ham, ko'rsatuv mazmuni ham), and in 1931 he televised the Epsom Derby horse race live.

EHowever, at the beginning of the 1930s, interest in television in Britain was spreading beyond Baird. The research departments of Britain’s two largest record companies came together and formed Electric and Musical Industries Ltd. (EMI) in 1931. 23The new department of brilliant scientists was led by Isaac Schoenberg(D — ovoz yozish kompaniyalari jamoasi rahbari), who, like Zworykin, had once worked in Russia. The group first looked to develop cathode-ray receivers, retaining mechanical scanning for generating the picture. However, work on an electronic camera tube started in 1933, after Zworykin’s paper was published.

FThe EMI team made a successful electronic tube in January 1934 and they called the design the Emitron. This was fundamentally the same as Zworykin’s Iconoscope, though there were manufacturing differences that gave it a superior performance. EMI did not immediately give up mechanical scanning, but they now joined forces with the Marconi Company to design the components and circuits of an all-electronic system of television. Very soon 18the technical superiority of the Marconi-EMI system was so obvious(F — elektron tizim ustun bo'lgan) that, on 30 January 1937, the BBC ended transmissions using Baird’s television equipment.

GIn 1936 television receiving sets were very expensive, 24possibly costing as much as a small car((small) car). Fewer than 500 were in use when the broadcasting service started, and growth was slower than had been hoped for. The signals could only be received clearly 25within a radius of about 30 miles(30 miles) from the television studio. Gradually the cost of sets came down, and just under 19,000 had been sold by 1939, when 19television in Britain abruptly came to an end for security reasons(G — xavfsizlik (urush) sababli). War was about to be declared. The screens were to be dark for nearly seven years until, in 1946, the British government decided to restart television.

HTransmissions restarted with a broadcast of the Victory Parade in London. Television remained confined to the London region until 1949 when 26studios in Birmingham opened(Birmingham). From then on, coverage gradually spread across Britain. Many people had their first experience of television watching Queen Elizabeth’s 1953 coronation ceremony. Households with television receivers opened up their homes to friends and neighbors. As the better-off purchased their new sets, so their old ones were sold on, which enlarged the audience still further. With quality entertainment now cheaply available at home, 16people went out far less often. Many cinemas and theatres closed down(H — kinoteatrlar yopilgan), and town centers became much emptier at night. The television age had finally arrived.

The psychology of new product adoption

John Gourville explores the reasons why consumers don't always buy new products

An American philosopher once said, 'If a man can make a better mousetrap than his neighbor, the world will make a beaten path to his door.' If only marketing innovation was that simple. In today's marketplace, companies that successfully introduce new products are more likely to flourish than those that don't. However, businesses spend billions of dollars making better 'mousetraps' only to find consumers rejecting them. Studies show that new products fail at the staggering rate of between 40% and 90%. In the US packaged goods industry, for example, companies introduce 30,000 new products every year, but 70% to 90% of them stay on store shelves for less than 12 months. According to one study, 2747% of the start-up companies that pioneered new products later went out of business altogether(A — 47% kompaniya yopilgan).

Experts and novices alike tend to dismiss unsuccessful innovations as bad ideas that were destined to fail. 28But surely that's too simple an explanation(D — sababi murakkabroq). If these innovations were so misguided, why wasn't it obvious from the outset? Why don't consumers buy innovative products with distinct advantages over existing ones? To understand why new products fail we must delve into the psychology of behavior change.

Companies have long assumed that 33they have only to develop innovations that are objectively superior to existing products, and consumers will have sufficient incentive to purchase them(B — yaxshiroq mahsulotni albatta sotib olishadi). In the 1960s, communications scholar Everett Rogers called the concept 'relative advantage' and identified it as the most critical driver of new-product adoption. Although compelling, the theory has one major flaw: 36it fails to capture the psychological biases that affect decision-making(NO — psixologik omillarni hisobga olmaydi).

In 2002, psychologist Daniel Kahneman won the Nobel Prize in economics 34for his research into the reasons why individuals deviate from rational economic behavior(A — kutilgan xarid modelidan chetlanish). One of the cornerstones of that research, developed with psychologist Amos Tversky, is how individuals value choices in the marketplace. Kahneman and Tversky showed that our responses to the alternatives before us have four distinct characteristics.

First, 35people evaluate the attractiveness of an alternative based not on its objective or actual value, but on its subjective or perceived value(D — subyektiv qiymat). Second, 29consumers evaluate new products relative to the products they already own(A — borlari bilan solishtiradi). Third, people view any improvements relative to this reference point and treat all shortcomings as losses. Fourth, and most important, losses have a far greater impact on people than similarly sized gains, a phenomenon that Kahneman and Tversky called 'loss aversion'. Loss aversion leads people to value products they already possess more than those that they don't. In other words, 37our desire to keep what we have is far greater than our desire to gain something new(YES — borini saqlash istagi kuchliroq).

In a 1990 paper, behavioral economist Richard Thaler and his colleagues described a series of experiments they conducted to quantify this phenomenon. In one experiment, they gave coffee mugs to a group of people, the 'Sellers', and asked at what price point, from 25 cents to $9.25, they would be willing to part with those mugs. The researchers then asked another group, the 'Choosers', to whom they didn't give coffee mugs, to indicate whether they would choose the money or the mug at each price point. In objective terms, all the Sellers and the Choosers were in the same situation: they were choosing between a mug and a sum of money. In one trial the Sellers priced the mug at $7.12, on average, but the Choosers were willing to pay only $3.12. In another trial, the Sellers and the Choosers valued the mug at $7 and $3.50, respectively. Overall, 30the Sellers always demanded at least twice as much to give up the mugs as the Choosers would pay to obtain them(B — Sotuvchilar ikki baravar qimmat baholagan).

In a 1989 paper, economist Jack Knetsch helped to explain why people tend to stick with what they have even if a better alternative exists, by providing a compelling demonstration of what is known as the 'status quo bias'. Knetsch asked one group of students to choose between an attractive coffee mug and a bar of Swiss chocolate. 31He gave a second group the coffee mugs but later allowed each student to exchange their mugs for a chocolate bar(B — borini yangisiga almashtirish taklifi). Finally, Knetsch gave chocolate bars to a third group then later allowed them to exchange their bar for a mug. Of the students initially given a choice, 56% chose the mug and 44% chose the chocolate bar, indicating a near even split in preferences between the two products. Logically, therefore, about half of the students to whom Knetsch gave the coffee mug should have traded for the chocolate bar and vice versa. However, in reality, 32only 11% of the students with mugs and 10% of those with chocolate bars wanted to exchange their products(C — ~90% almashtirmagan). To approximately 90% of the students, giving up what they already had seemed like a painful loss and reduced their desire to trade.

Interestingly, most people seem oblivious to the existence of the behaviors implicit in these biases. In study after study, when researchers presented people with evidence that they had irrationally overvalued what they had, they were shocked, skeptical and more than a little defensive. These behavioral tendencies are universal, but awareness of them is not.

In a perfect world, companies would know that consumers irrationally overvalue incumbent products and would take that bias into account when launching innovations. But developers are also biased in favor of new products. 39Having worked on a new product for years(NOT GIVEN — vaqt behuda ketishi haqida fikr yo'q), developers operate in a world where their innovation is the reference point. 38They're convinced that the product works(NOT GIVEN — har doim muvaffaqiyat qozonishiga ishonishi aytilmagan) and they are keenly aware of the shortcomings of existing alternatives. Companies call those developers 'product champions' or 'believers', 40suggesting that they have embraced a world the rest of us haven't yet(NO — 'product champion' mahsulotga ishonuvchi, eng ko'p sotiladigan mahsulot yaratuvchi emas).
